# Principles of Biblical Interpretation

*Together Before Scripture — Principles of Biblical Interpretation and Commentary*

## How We Read Scripture

This project approaches the Bible as Scripture written through human authors under divine inspiration. Yet readers are not required to share that belief before exploring its message. We first seek to understand what the biblical authors intended to communicate within their historical, linguistic, and literary contexts, while recognizing that each reader must decide whether to accept that message.

## Nine Principles of Biblical Interpretation

The following nine principles guide how this project reads Scripture and develops its commentaries. Each principle is explained in more detail in its corresponding section below.

1. We first seek to understand what Scripture communicates
2. We begin with the author's historical and cultural setting
3. We acknowledge both the integrity and the limits of human authors
4. We trust Scripture's truthfulness without forcing historical harmonizations
5. We distinguish textual evidence, interpretation, inference, and application
6. We do not treat any particular manuscript or translation as infallible
7. We establish the original meaning before examining canonical connections
8. We also examine the history of those who preserved and transmitted Scripture
9. Commentaries are open to continued examination and revision

## 1. We first seek to understand what Scripture communicates

I believe that the Bible is the Word of God, written by human authors under divine inspiration. But I do not require every reader to share that belief before beginning to read it.

Whether someone is a committed Christian, has left the faith, or does not believe in God, they can approach Scripture seriously while remaining open to the possibility that it conveys God's Word. Just as we first try to understand an author's argument before deciding whether to accept it, we should first seek to understand Scripture's message on its own terms.

**Understanding what Scripture communicates and accepting it as one's own belief are distinct matters.** Each reader must make that decision.

## 2. We begin with the author's historical and cultural setting

Scripture was written within human history and language. Its authors lived in particular cultures and periods and communicated through the languages and literary conventions available to them. We therefore begin with an author's historical circumstances, language, literary genre, immediate context, and intended original audience.

We seek not to project modern assumptions onto ancient texts or attribute meanings that their authors did not intend. **Our first question is not, “What does this passage mean to me?” but, “What was its author trying to communicate to the original audience?”** Only after examining that meaning do we consider theological and ethical implications for today.

## 3. We acknowledge both the integrity and the limits of human authors

I believe that the biblical authors sincerely sought to communicate the truth entrusted to them through divine inspiration. I do not begin by assuming that they intended to deceive their readers or invent false accounts.

Yet inspiration does not imply that an author possessed omniscience or powers of expression beyond every human limitation. The authors expressed what they knew, experienced, or received through revelation using the cognitive, linguistic, and cultural resources of their time. One author could emphasize an aspect of an event while another emphasized something different. The four Gospels illustrate this well: their authors bear witness to the same Jesus Christ while selecting, highlighting, and arranging different material. **A partial perspective or selective account is not, in itself, a falsehood or distortion.** We should evaluate an author's expressions in light of their historical language and literary conventions, without invoking human limitations to dismiss what a passage actually asserts.

## 4. We trust Scripture's truthfulness without forcing historical harmonizations

Believing that Scripture communicates truth is not the same as being certain that my interpretation of a particular event is correct. For example, John places Jesus' cleansing of the temple near the beginning of his public ministry, while the Synoptic Gospels place it during his final visit to Jerusalem. We may consider both the possibility that Jesus cleansed the temple twice and the possibility that the Gospel writers arranged one event differently for literary purposes.

The two-event explanation takes John's apparent chronology more directly, while the one-event explanation can be examined in light of ancient historiographical practices and each Gospel's theological aims. I will not ignore contrary evidence merely to favor one explanation, or invent an unreported event solely to preserve a preferred harmonization.

**If the available evidence does not justify a firm conclusion, I will leave the question open.** That does not mean doubting Scripture's truthfulness; it means distinguishing Scripture's truthfulness from confidence in my own interpretation.

## 5. We distinguish textual evidence, interpretation, inference, and application

When reading Scripture, I seek to distinguish:

- **What the text directly states:** The claims and events explicitly communicated by the author
- **Historical and literary interpretation:** Meanings assessed using language, context, culture, and relevant evidence
- **Possible inferences:** Explanations compatible with the text but not directly established by it
- **Theological and personal application:** Judgments about how the passage relates to belief and life today

When several interpretations are plausible, I will compare their principal arguments and explain how confidently each can be held. I do not rule out divine intervention or miracles in advance. Nor do I invent symbolic or mystical meanings unrelated to the passage's historical and literary context.

## 6. We do not treat any particular manuscript or translation as infallible

I trust the truthfulness of what God communicated through the biblical authors. But I do not believe that every surviving manuscript or translation produced during transmission is flawless. Scripture has been copied and translated through many centuries and communities, and textual variants arose in that process. Rather than deny those differences, I want to compare manuscript evidence and early translations to assess which readings are better supported.

For the **Old Testament, the Masoretic Text is our starting point**, while the Septuagint and Dead Sea Scrolls are important witnesses to alternative readings. Passages such as Deuteronomy 32:8 may warrant revisiting the initial textual choice. For the **New Testament, we begin with the Textus Receptus (TR), used extensively by the 1611 KJV translators, and the closely related Byzantine textual tradition**, while also examining Alexandrian and other manuscript traditions. The TR and Byzantine text are not identical. When variants materially change the meaning of a passage, I will assess their manuscript evidence and historical and grammatical contexts, considering both the effect on that passage and **whether the differences have implications for Scripture's broader message and major theological teachings**. I value the KJV, but do not regard all its textual and translation decisions as beyond correction.

**Trusting God's Word is compatible with critically examining manuscripts and translations in order to understand it more faithfully.**

## 7. We establish the original meaning before examining canonical connections

I believe that the Old and New Testaments together form one canon bearing witness to God's redemptive work. Yet I do not begin by making every Old Testament event correspond to a New Testament person or episode, or by setting aside an Old Testament passage's original historical context to find a reference to Jesus Christ.

I first examine what a passage meant to its author and original audience. I then consider how later biblical writings cite or develop it and assess its canonical and redemptive-historical significance. Christological and typological interpretations also require evidence in the text and canon. **Later theological meaning should not replace the passage's original historical and literary meaning.**

## 8. We also examine the history of those who preserved and transmitted Scripture

Scripture is not merely an ancient collection of writings. It also comes to us through the history of people who believed its message was true and worked to preserve and pass it on. Some early Christians faced persecution and death rather than renounce their faith; people in later generations also undertook risks to copy, preserve, and translate Scripture. I regard that history as important evidence when evaluating Scripture's credibility.

What did they believe was worth risking their lives for? What did they consider true, and through which writings was that message transmitted? Martyrdom does not automatically prove every claim those believers held, but **it can provide strong evidence that they sincerely believed their faith was true**. The history of textual transmission also enables us to investigate how the message they sought to preserve reached later generations. In assessing Scripture's historical credibility, I therefore consider not only manuscripts and dates but also the lives and sacrifices of those who bore witness to and transmitted its message.

## 9. Commentaries are open to continued examination and revision

The commentaries published here present my current understanding from studying and reading Scripture. I will begin with a passage's meaning and historical setting, explain important interpretive questions and alternatives where appropriate, and distinguish evidence from inference rather than conceal uncertainty.

When new evidence or stronger arguments warrant a change, I will revise earlier interpretations. Such revision is part of seeking a more faithful understanding, not an effort to conceal previous mistakes. I welcome readers' questions and criticism, and will document significant revisions and their reasons where possible.

## Standing together before Scripture

I once left Christianity and later returned to faith through reading Scripture. What mattered in that process was not having someone impose a conclusion on me in advance, but **first discovering what Scripture actually says**. Together Before Scripture grew out of a desire to share that way of reading.

I believe that the Bible is God's Word. But I do not ask readers to begin by sharing that belief. Let us first read carefully, seeking the message its authors intended for their own audiences. Let us ask questions, consider the evidence, and reexamine our assumptions and interpretations. Each reader can then decide, after serious engagement, whether the message is true and what significance it may have for their life.

**Read together, ask together.**
